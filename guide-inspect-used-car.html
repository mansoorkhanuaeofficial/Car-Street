How to inspect a used car before you buy it

Updated 29 September 2026

A used car can be a great deal or an expensive lesson

The difference usually shows up in the first hour of checking

This guide follows the same steps a careful buyer takes in any of our countries

Start with the paperwork

Ask to see the registration document and the service record before you look at the car

Both should show the same owner name and the same vehicle identification number

Ask for a history report from an official or trusted provider in your country

Match the numbers

The number appears on the dashboard near the windscreen and on the driver door frame

Check that both match the paperwork

A mismatch is a reason to stop and ask questions

Read the service record

Regular oil changes and timing belt work matter more than a polished interior

A gap of two years or more deserves a direct question to the seller

Walk around the car

Body and panels

Look along each side in daylight

Uneven gaps between panels or paint that changes shade can point to a past repair

Rust bubbles near wheel arches and door edges spread quickly

Tyres and brakes

Tyres should wear evenly across the tread

Uneven wear can mean alignment or suspension trouble

Check brake discs for deep grooves or a heavy lip on the edge

Look under the bonnet or hood

Do this with a cold engine because a warm engine can hide hard starting problems

Check the oil level and colour since milky oil suggests coolant is leaking into the engine

Look for damp patches around hoses and the radiator

Listen at startup for knocking or a rattle

Take a proper test drive

Drive for at least 20 minutes on mixed roads and include a stretch at motorway or highway speed

Test every window and light and switch

Warning lights should appear at startup and then go out

A light that never comes on may have been disabled

On a straight road the car should hold its line with your hands loose

Brake firmly once and check that it stops without pulling to one side

Automatic gearboxes should shift smoothly and quickly

Listen for humming from the wheels

Know when to walk away

Some problems are cheap to fix and others aren't

Walk away if the seller refuses an independent inspection or rushes you

Walk away too if the history report shows unexplained mileage changes or major accident damage

Consider paying a local mechanic or a motoring club for a full inspection

It usually costs far less than one surprise repair

Rules differ between countries so check your local requirements for ownership transfer and roadworthiness certificates
